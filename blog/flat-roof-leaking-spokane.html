Why Flat Roofs on Spokane Homes Leak

What actually causes it, why the stain is never where the hole is, and how to fix it for good.

Fluid Roofing · Spokane & Airway Heights, WA

Flat roofs are more common on Spokane homes than people expect - mid-century houses, additions, garages, carports, porch covers, and dormers. They work fine when they are built and maintained right. When they leak, though, they tend to leak in ways that confuse homeowners, because the wet spot on your ceiling is often nowhere near the actual hole.

First: your flat roof is not actually flat

Every flat roof is built with a slight slope so water runs off to a drain, scupper, or gutter. It is usually too gentle to see from the ground. That slope is the whole design. When it gets compromised - by a sagging deck, a settled section, or a clogged drain - water stops leaving and starts sitting.

The four things that actually cause leaks

Ponding water. Water still standing a day or two after rain is the number one enemy of a low-slope roof. It works into seams, breaks down the membrane, and adds weight.

Failed seams. A flat roof is made of sheets joined together, and those joins are the weak point. On older systems the adhesive or asphalt at the seams dries out, shrinks, and opens up.

Flashing. Anywhere the roof meets something else - a wall, a vent, a skylight, a chimney - there is flashing. Most leaks we find start at one of these transitions, not out in the open field of the roof.

Age and sun. UV breaks roofing material down over time. A membrane that has gone brittle, chalky, or cracked is at the end of its service life no matter how it looks from the driveway.

Why the water shows up in the wrong place

On a pitched roof, water runs downhill and shows up more or less below where it got in. On a flat roof, water gets under the membrane and travels sideways across the deck until it finds a seam, a fastener, or a low spot to drip through. That is why chasing a ceiling stain with a bucket of patch material so often fails - you are patching where the water lands, not where it entered.

Patch, coat, or replace?

The honest answer depends on how much life is left in the system underneath.

A patch makes sense for a single, identifiable failure on a roof that is otherwise sound. It buys years, not decades.

A coating works when the existing roof is intact but weathered. A fluid-applied coating seals the surface and the seams and adds service life, often for less than a tear-off. It is a large part of what we do - see how roof coatings work.

A replacement is the answer when the deck is wet, the insulation is saturated, or the membrane has failed across the whole roof. Once water is in the decking, sealing the top just traps it.

The one thing worth doing today: after the next real rain, look at your flat roof and see whether water is still standing 48 hours later.
